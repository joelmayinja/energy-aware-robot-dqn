Energy-Aware Control of a Simulated KUKA iiwa

An evaluation of an inverse-kinematics-assisted Deep Q-Network (DQN)

Mayinja Joel · BSc Computer Science · Uganda Christian University

Research questionCan the learned controller reach targets while reducing simulated grid energy against an inverse-kinematics baseline?
What the data say

The selected DQN reached targets in 44/50 held-out episodes (88%), versus 45/50 (90%) for IK. In matched successes, the DQN used more energy on average. Target reaching was demonstrated; energy saving was not.

Why this matters

Robot control must balance task success with energy use. Reinforcement learning research highlights that directly penalizing energy can conflict with task performance [1]. Deep reinforcement learning has also been applied to motion planning and energy-oriented inverse kinematics for redundant manipulators [2].

This prototype measures that trade-off in a controlled simulation. It does not claim industrial or physical-robot performance.

System and method

  • PyBullet simulation of a KUKA iiwa; six joints controlled, wrist roll locked.
  • 23-value observation: joint state, end-effector and target positions, task phase, buffer level, grid draw, distance, and episode progress.
  • 89 discrete actions built from single-joint velocity levels and coordinated joint directions.
  • IK demonstrations initialize the DQN; DQN fine-tuning uses replay, a target network, and epsilon-greedy exploration.
  • Each episode has a light target then a harder target; success threshold is 0.15 m and episode limit is 200 steps.

Energy model

Positive simulated joint mechanical work counts as grid draw. Negative work during braking may charge a buffer with a 50 Ws capacity and 80% charging efficiency, for use in the harder phase.

This simplified model is not a measured battery, motor, or factory power model.

How to read this result

Completion is similar, with IK ahead by one episode. The selected DQN did not reduce energy in matched successful cases. The evidence therefore supports target-reaching capability in this simulation, not energy efficiency.

Interpretation

The current result is useful because it exposes a gap between reaching the target and meeting the energy objective. Lower energy cannot be inferred from reward or completion alone; it must be measured against the baseline on comparable successful episodes.

Limitations

  • Simulation only; no physical KUKA experiment.
  • Energy recovery and the buffer are simplified assumptions.
  • Energy comparison is restricted to 44 seeds where both controllers succeeded.
  • One selected checkpoint and 50 held-out seeds provide limited evidence about generalization.

Conclusion and next steps

In this evaluation, the IK-assisted DQN reached 88% of targets and the IK baseline reached 90%. The DQN used more energy on average in matched successes. The energy-saving objective remains unvalidated.

Next: examine reward and action design, repeat training with multiple random seeds, and retain paired success-and-energy reporting before claiming any improvement.
